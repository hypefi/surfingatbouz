If you are planning a trip and typing **surfing between Rabat and Casablanca Atlantic coast** into a search bar, you are looking at one of the most practical surf stretches in Morocco. It sits on the doorstep of two big cities, it has real beaches and real waves, and you can reach it without a long southbound journey.

We are a small school in Bouznika, about halfway along this coast, and we surf and teach here all year. This guide is what we would tell a friend who messaged us before arriving: how the coast works, what kind of surfer it suits, what to pack, and how to plan your days.

## What the coast between Rabat and Casablanca is actually like

Picture a long, mostly open Atlantic shoreline running between two cities. Between them are fishing towns, beach resorts, river mouths, rocky headlands and wide stretches of sand. It does not have one famous wave that appears on every poster. It has many beaches that behave differently depending on the tide, the wind and the swell direction.

That variety is the first thing to understand. Some beaches are open and exposed, some are more sheltered by headlands, and some change shape every season as the sandbanks move. A beach that was perfect for a lesson last week may be messy this week, and the next one along the road may be better. This is why local knowledge matters more here than a list of "best spots".

### Beach breaks dominate

Most of what you will surf on this stretch is **beach break**: waves that break over sand rather than over a reef. For learners that is good news. Falling onto sand and whitewater is far kinder than falling onto rock, and you can stand in the water to practise.

The trade-off is that sandbanks shift, so the waves are less predictable than a classic point. You learn to read the water, find the channel where the current pulls you back out, and pick the peak that is actually breaking cleanly.

### Rocks, reefs and headlands

Not everywhere is sand. There are rocky sections and reef-type setups along the Atlantic coast, and these are not first-day waves. If you see a beautiful wave breaking over rocks, ask before you paddle. A good rule for visitors: if you are still working on your pop-up, choose sand and ask a local to confirm.

## The towns along the stretch

We will not pretend to give you a spot-by-spot rating, because conditions change too much. Instead, here is how we think about it.

**Near Rabat.** The capital side of the coast has city beaches and surf communities close to town. It is convenient if you are staying in Rabat for work or sightseeing and want a session before or after.

**The middle.** This is where we are. Bouznika is a quieter coastal town, with a wide sandy beach, and it is easy to reach from both cities. It is a good base for people who want to surf rather than sightsee.

**Near Casablanca.** The Casablanca side has more urban surf, more people in the water, and a different feel. Our overview of [where to surf near Casablanca](/blog/where-to-surf-near-casablanca) covers that side in more detail.

## When to go

The Atlantic coast of Morocco can be surfed all year, but the character changes with the seasons.

### Summer

Summer is typically **smaller and cleaner**, which is exactly what first-timers want. The water is warmer, the days are long, and sessions are more relaxed. It is also when the coast is busiest with holidaymakers, so lineups and parking can feel fuller on weekends.

### Autumn and spring

These shoulder seasons are a sweet spot for many people. You get a mix of conditions, fewer crowds than summer, and enough variety to keep progressing surfers interested. Mornings can be cool, so bring layers for the beach.

### Winter

Winter is **punchier**. The ocean is stronger, the water is cold, and some days are not suitable for beginners at all. Experienced surfers love it. If you are new, plan lessons around the best windows and be flexible, because we will sometimes move a session to a calmer day.

We do not publish swell tables, because a number on a chart does not tell you whether a beach is fun. We would rather tell you what to expect in general and then check the water on the morning.

## Planning a trip along this coast

### Getting there

Both cities have good road and rail connections, so you can arrive in one and surf in the other. Many visitors fly into Casablanca, take a train toward Rabat, and then continue to the coast by taxi or a rented car. If you are not sure about the last leg, message us before you travel and we will tell you what is easiest.

### Where to base yourself

You have two reasonable approaches.

1. **Pick one base and stay.** This is best if you are learning. Repetition builds skills faster than moving every day, and you get to know a single beach well.
2. **Move along the coast.** This suits surfers who already stand up and want to compare beaches. A guide helps here, because they know which beach is working on which day.

### What to bring

- A swimsuit and a rash vest or t-shirt for sun protection
- Sunscreen, a hat and sunglasses for the beach
- A towel and a change of clothes
- Water and a snack
- A wetsuit if you have one, since you save 50 dh on lessons
- Flip-flops, because sand gets hot in summer

If you are bringing a board from abroad, check it before you fly. Small dings are cheap to fix at the start of a trip and expensive to ignore. Our [board repair workshop](/board-repair) can look at it.

## Etiquette and safety on a busy coast

A coast near two big cities means other people are in the water. A few habits make everyone's day easier.

- **Respect priority.** The surfer closest to the breaking part of the wave has priority. If you are unsure, wait.
- **Do not drop in.** Taking off on someone already riding the wave is the quickest way to start a bad day.
- **Control your board.** Hold on to it in whitewater and never let it fly toward people.
- **Learn about currents.** Beach breaks have rips that pull water back out. They can help you paddle out, but they can also carry you farther than you planned. Ask a local where they are.
- **Know your limit.** If the waves look bigger than you are ready for, choose another beach or another day.

None of this is complicated, but it matters more when a beach is shared by beginners, locals and visitors.

## A realistic first trip

If we were designing a short trip for someone new to surfing, we would keep it simple. Spend three or four days in one place, have a lesson each morning, rest in the afternoon and let your arms recover. You will improve quickly, because the beach is familiar and the coach knows what you worked on yesterday.

If you already surf, spend a day or two exploring with someone local, then choose a favourite and go back. The reward on this stretch is not one iconic wave. It is finding a beach that works for you, on a day that suits you, and having space to enjoy it.
