If you are trying to start surfing, the single skill that decides how fun your first weeks feel is the pop-up. This guide covers **learning to pop up on a surfboard in small Atlantic waves**, the way we teach it on our beach in Bouznika, between Rabat and Casablanca. We are not going to promise you will stand on every wave by Friday. We will tell you what actually works, what usually goes wrong, and how to practise so the time you spend in the water counts.

## What the pop-up actually is

The pop-up is the transition from lying on the board, paddling, to standing in a surf stance. It sounds like one move. In reality it is three small ones strung together: you catch the wave, you push your upper body up, and you bring your feet under you. Beginners often try to do all three at once and end up in a tangle. We separate them first and then let them run together.

A good pop-up has a few simple qualities:

- It is **fast but not frantic**. You want speed from your arms and hips, not a panic.
- Your **eyes stay forward**, on the beach or the wave, not on your feet.
- Your **hands push the board down** and your chest lifts before your feet move.
- Your feet land **wide and balanced** over the middle of the board.

Once those four things are in your head, the rest is practice.

## Learning to pop up on a surfboard in small Atlantic waves: why small is better

A lot of beginners assume bigger waves mean faster learning. It is the opposite. The pop-up is a coordination skill, and coordination is learned when you can try again quickly and calmly.

The waves we teach in are usually small, soft and close to the shore. Whitewater that has already broken pushes the board forward gently and gives you a few extra seconds to move. On a steeper wave you need to be quicker and more certain, which is unforgiving for a first-timer.

Bouznika is a sandy beach, so a fall mostly means a mouthful of salt rather than a scrape on rock. That matters for confidence. When falling is not scary, you take more tries, and more tries is how you learn. If you want to see how the beach compares with other spots, our guide to [Bouznika versus Taghazout for beginners](/blog/bouznika-vs-taghazout-for-beginners) explains why we think sandy beaches suit new surfers.

Conditions change with the season and the day, so we do not give fixed numbers. What we can say from teaching here is that smaller, softer days are the days we are happiest to see a beginner, and we pick the spot in the water to match the surfer in front of us.

## Step one: the pop-up on the sand

We always start on dry sand, before anyone gets wet. This is not a formality. Practising out of the water removes the balance problem and lets you focus on the order of movements.

### Lie down in the right spot

Lie on the board on your stomach, chest slightly lifted, with the board's centre under your belly and your toes near the tail. Too far forward and the nose digs. Too far back and you cannot get the board moving. In the water we adjust you by a hand's width until the board sits level.

### Hands and chest

Place your hands flat on the board beside your ribs, fingers pointing forward, like the start of a push-up. Do not grip the rails with your hands. Gripping the rails is the most common beginner habit and it ruins the push because your elbows end up out to the sides.

### Push, then step

Push your chest up with your arms straight. In one fluid move, bring your back foot under your hips and then place the front foot between your hands. Your feet should land roughly shoulder-width apart, with the front foot pointing forward and the back foot turned slightly across the board. Then stand tall, knees bent, arms loose in front of you.

We repeat this slowly, then at a normal speed, then with a partner calling "wave" so you practise reacting. The aim is a movement your body does without you narrating it.

### Check your stance

Your stance is either regular, with the left foot forward, or goofy, with the right foot forward. We find out by a gentle test: stand naturally and have someone give you a light nudge from behind. The foot you step forward with to catch yourself is usually your front foot. Neither stance is better. If you are unsure, we choose one on the sand and change it if the water says otherwise.

## Step two: paddling and catching a small wave

You cannot pop up on a wave you did not catch. A common pattern is that beginners have a decent pop-up but never get moving, because their paddling is timid.

In small waves this is simple. Lie centred on the board, keep your head up, and paddle with long, deep strokes. When the wave arrives behind you, paddle harder for two or three strokes, not fewer. Many beginners stop paddling the moment they feel the wave touch the tail. That is exactly when you need one more push so the board is travelling with the wave and not just being lifted by it.

When you feel the board accelerate, stop paddling, place your hands, and begin the pop-up. We call out the timing from the beach side in the early lessons, so you are not guessing.

## Step three: the pop-up in the whitewater

Whitewater is where the pop-up becomes real. Here is how we run the first water session:

1. **Walk out** to waist-deep water with the board, holding it by the rails with the nose pointing at the shore.
2. **Wait for a broken wave** coming toward you and turn the board to face the beach.
3. **Climb on** and lie in your sand-practised position.
4. **Paddle** with the coach pushing or calling the timing at first.
5. **Pop up** as soon as the board is moving forward.
6. **Fall safely.** Fall flat to the side, away from the board, and cover your head with your arms when you surface.

You will fall a lot in the first few attempts. That is not failure; it is the process. When a student gets up, even for two seconds, we stop and repeat the same thing, because repetition is what builds the movement.

## The most common pop-up mistakes (and quick fixes)

Almost every beginner makes the same handful of mistakes. Knowing them in advance saves you a few frustrating sessions.

### Looking down at the board

When you look at your feet your weight drifts forward and the nose dives. Keep your head up and look where you want to go. This one correction fixes more falls than any equipment change.

### Using your knees as a staging post

Many beginners get to their knees first because it feels safer. The problem is that you lose speed and by the time you stand the wave has passed. We encourage you to go from lying to a low stand in one movement, even if it is awkward at first.

### Feet too close together

A narrow stance makes the board feel tippy. Think of standing on a bus that is braking: feet apart, knees soft. A wider base gives you balance without extra effort.

### Standing too tall, too soon

After the pop-up, stay low. Bent knees act as shock absorbers. Standing straight up on the first wave is like standing up in a canoe. Let the movement settle before you rise.

### Hands on the rails

As said earlier, gripping the rails stops you from pushing properly. Hands flat, fingers forward, push through the palms.

### Rushing the pop-up before the board moves

If you try to stand while the board is still stationary, it will wobble and you will fall. Wait for the push of the wave, then go. That small pause is a skill in itself.

## Drills you can do before and between lessons

You do not need a beach to improve your pop-up. These simple drills help.

- **Floor pop-ups.** Mark a board-shaped rectangle on the floor with tape. Lie in it and practise the movement slowly. Check your feet land inside the lines.
- **Mirror check.** Do the pop-up in front of a mirror and watch your head position. Is your chin up and your chest open?
- **Slow-motion rep.** Do the pop-up in five seconds, pausing at each stage. Slow reps reveal where you are rushing.
- **Core and shoulder basics.** A few push-ups and a plank a few times a week make the paddling and the push easier. Nothing extreme, just regular.
- **Balance work.** Standing on one leg while brushing your teeth sounds silly and helps your balance more than you would expect.

Short, regular practice beats a long session once a week.

## Fitness, age and fear

People ask whether they are too old, too heavy or too nervous. In our experience the answer is almost always that the pop-up can be adapted. A stiff back may mean a slightly different foot placement. Shoulder pain means we change how you push. Fear of the water means we stay in shallow whitewater until you are comfortable. If something hurts, tell your coach on the beach and we adjust.

The mental part is often bigger than the physical part. Many beginners hesitate because they imagine the fall. The best trick is to decide in advance that falling is part of the session and to fall on purpose a few times, flat and away from the board. Once falling stops being scary, standing up gets easier.

## After the pop-up: what comes next

Once you can stand on a broken wave reliably, the next steps are small, and they are fun:

- Riding straight toward the beach with a steady stance.
- Learning to look and lean slightly to turn.
- Paddling a little further out to catch unbroken, gentler waves.
- Reading the beach: where the waves break, where the current pulls, and when to stay out.

Do not skip ahead. A solid pop-up makes every one of these steps easier.
